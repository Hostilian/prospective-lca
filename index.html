Executive summary

This preview shows the structure and logic of a prospective life cycle assessment for grape pomace valorisation in Portugal, using illustrative numbers in place of real site data. Under the illustrative assumptions, climate-related impacts from processing one tonne of wet pomace could fall between 25% and 82% compared with a 2025 reference case, depending on how aggressively the process is improved and how quickly Portugal’s electricity grid decarbonises — but those figures are driven by synthetic inputs and must not be used for investment or policy decisions.

The dominant source of climate impact in the baseline is grid electricity, which contributes approximately 80% of the total. This means interventions that reduce electricity consumption or switch to a lower-carbon supply will have the largest effect. A real LCA study would replace every synthetic number with measured or verified data, apply an approved impact assessment method, and quantify the uncertainty around each result.

The immediate next step is a scoping conversation to agree the decision question, the process boundary, and the data you can supply. Section 10 lists exactly what we would need.

What this study covers

Functional unit

The functional unit (FU) — the reference quantity to which every result is normalised — is one tonne of wet grape pomace entering the valorisation process. To estimate the total impact of a full harvest season, multiply any result by the annual throughput in tonnes.

System boundary

The study covers the conversion stage only, from the point where pomace arrives at the valorisation facility to the point where products leave it (gate-to-gate). This includes:

  • Grid electricity consumed by the process
  • Process heat (thermal energy for drying or hydrolysis)
  • Process water consumption
  • Enzyme preparation used in enzymatic hydrolysis
  • Transport of the pomace feedstock to the facility
  • A credit for the mineral fertiliser that the recovered material would replace if applied to agricultural land (an avoided-burden credit — see Assumptions)

Grape growing, wine production, and the end-of-life of recovered products are outside the boundary. Expanding the boundary would change the results and must be agreed before a real study begins.

System boundary diagram

Geography and years

All calculations use Portuguese electricity and transport data (or their illustrative proxies). The baseline represents 2025 conditions. The four future scenarios project to 2030 and 2040, assuming Portugal’s electricity mix evolves along different decarbonisation trajectories.

Counterfactual

A prospective LCA must state what currently happens to the material being studied — the counterfactual. In Portugal, grape pomace is primarily processed for marc brandy (aguardente) distillation; composting and animal feed are secondary routes; some goes to landfill. This study does not yet compare valorisation against a specific counterfactual. Defining the counterfactual is a required step before any real LCA can be finalised.

The five scenarios

One baseline and four future scenarios are compared. Each future scenario combines assumptions about process efficiency changes (the foreground — what happens inside the facility) and about how Portugal’s electricity grid changes (the background — the carbon intensity of the power supply). All assumptions are illustrative and proposed, not confirmed.

Reference
Baseline 2025
Year: 2025

The starting point. The process uses 420 kWh of electricity and 180 kWh of process heat per tonne of pomace, at an illustrative 2025 Portuguese grid carbon intensity of 0.42 kg CO₂e per kWh. No efficiency improvements are assumed. The avoided fertiliser credit is based on 0.35 t of mineral fertiliser equivalent replaced per tonne of pomace.

2030 · Central
Central pathway
Year: 2030

Moderate process improvements — electricity use down 18%, heat use down 15%, enzyme dose down 10% — combined with a cleaner grid (illustrative: 0.24 kg CO₂e per kWh, a 43% reduction). A credible but not exceptional pace of change in both technology and energy policy.

2030 · Conservative
Conservative pathway
Year: 2030

Limited foreground improvement — electricity use down 5%, heat use down 8%, enzyme dose down 2% — and modest grid progress (illustrative: 0.32 kg CO₂e per kWh, a 24% reduction). Technology adoption is slow and the energy transition lags.

2040 · Conservative
Conservative pathway
Year: 2040

By 2040, even the conservative path reaches larger process improvements (electricity use down 20%, heat use down 20%), but the grid decarbonises only modestly (illustrative: 0.30 kg CO₂e per kWh). Progress is real but constrained by cautious investment and slow policy change.

2040 · Ambitious
Ambitious pathway
Year: 2040

Strong efficiency gains — electricity use down 40%, heat use down 35%, water reuse up 25% — combined with a near-clean grid (illustrative: 0.12 kg CO₂e per kWh, a 71% reduction) and a larger fertiliser credit from higher recovery. This represents aggressive technology learning and a high-ambition energy transition.

What drives the reductions

In a prospective LCA, a key question is: how much of the projected improvement comes from changing the process itself (the foreground — under the operator’s control) versus from using a cleaner electricity grid (the background — which depends on national energy policy)?

This decomposition is not available from the current synthetic data. The calculations change both foreground and background simultaneously in each scenario, so the two effects cannot currently be separated from the total.

What would be needed to show foreground vs. background

  1. Foreground-only variant: apply process efficiency changes but keep electricity factors at their 2025 values — shows the impact of operational improvements alone.
  2. Background-only variant: keep process quantities at baseline levels but update electricity factors to the scenario year — shows the impact of grid decarbonisation alone.
  3. Interaction term: the small remainder when both effects are combined.

The current data structure already separates foreground and background items, so this analysis is feasible without structural changes. It would require running two additional variants per scenario.

[ASSUMPTION] As an indicative estimate: because grid electricity contributes roughly 80% of baseline climate impact and the illustrative grid factor drops from 0.42 to 0.12 kg CO₂e/kWh in the ambitious 2040 scenario (a 71% reduction), a large share of the climate reduction in that scenario is likely attributable to grid decarbonisation rather than process improvements. This should be computed, not assumed.

Limitations and how to read these results

What these results are

These are the outputs of a workflow demonstration: illustrative calculations showing how a prospective LCA would be structured, what inputs it needs, and what kinds of results it could produce. The numbers are internally consistent but not connected to real measured data.

What these results are not

  • Not validated LCIA results. No approved life cycle impact assessment method has been applied. The three indicators are proxies.
  • Not ISO 14040/14044-compliant. The study does not meet the requirements for a published LCA, including critical review, data quality assessment, or peer verification.
  • Not decision-grade information. Do not use these numbers to justify investment, regulatory, or procurement decisions.
  • No uncertainty analysis. Every number is a single point estimate. The true result for a real process could differ substantially; a real study would quantify this uncertainty.
  • No sensitivity analysis. The effect of varying individual assumptions — for example, removing the fertiliser credit, or using a slower grid decarbonisation trajectory — has not been tested.

What would change these results

  • Using real site data (measured electricity, heat, water, and enzyme quantities)
  • Applying a published, versioned LCIA method
  • Using peer-reviewed prospective electricity background projections
  • Defining and computing the counterfactual (what currently happens to this pomace)
  • Verifying the fertiliser credit size and treatment method
  • Adding uncertainty and sensitivity analysis

What we need from you

Information required for a real study

  1. Decision question: What exactly are you trying to decide? For example: “Should we invest in enzymatic valorisation by 2030 rather than continuing to send pomace to distillation?”
  2. Process data: Measured or estimated electricity consumption (kWh per tonne pomace), heat demand, water use, enzyme purchase quantities, and transport distances to the facility.
  3. Current pomace route: Where does your pomace currently go? (Distillation, composting, animal feed, landfill, or a mix.) This defines the counterfactual.
  4. Recovered product: What does the valorisation process produce (extract, compost, biogas, other)? What is its nutrient or energy content? This determines whether and how a fertiliser or energy credit is justified.
  5. Scale: Annual throughput in tonnes and whether the process is at pilot, demonstration, or commercial scale.
  6. Target years: Which future years are relevant to your decision (2030, 2035, 2040)?
  7. Confidentiality requirements: What data can be published or shared, and what must remain confidential?

Proposed workflow

  1. Scoping meeting (approximately 1 hour) — agree the decision question, boundary, and data requirements.
  2. Data collection — you supply site measurements or estimates; we identify gaps requiring literature values or engineering estimates.
  3. Methodology agreement — agree the LCIA method, background database, scenario definitions, and credit treatment before calculating.
  4. Calculation and review — we run the calculations, share a draft, and invite your review before finalising.
  5. Final report — a fully documented result with uncertainty analysis, sensitivity analysis, and a clear statement of what the results do and do not support.

Glossary

Life Cycle Assessment (LCA)
A method for estimating the environmental impacts of a product or process across its full life cycle. Governed by ISO 14040 and ISO 14044.
Life Cycle Impact Assessment (LCIA)
The step within LCA that converts inventory flows (kg of CO₂, kWh of electricity, m³ of water) into environmental impact scores using published characterisation factors. Examples: ReCiPe 2016, CML-IA, EF 3.1.
Functional unit (FU)
The reference quantity to which all results are normalised. In this study: one tonne of wet grape pomace processed.
System boundary
The line drawn around what is included and excluded. This study uses a gate-to-gate boundary covering only the conversion stage.
Foreground
The processes directly controlled by the operator (electricity use, heat, water, enzymes). Foreground data are measured or estimated from the actual process.
Background
The upstream systems supplying inputs (electricity grid, transport network, raw material production). Background data come from LCA databases such as ecoinvent.
Prospective (ex-ante) LCA
An LCA that considers how future changes in both the process and the background system affect environmental outcomes, in contrast to an LCA of current technology with today’s grid.
Scenario
A coherent, internally consistent set of assumptions about future conditions. Scenarios are not predictions; they explore a credible range of futures.
Proxy indicator
An impact metric that approximates a real LCIA category without using an approved characterisation method. Used here because no LCIA method has been applied.
Avoided-burden credit
A credit applied when a co-product or recovered material replaces another product that would otherwise have been produced, thereby avoiding its environmental impact.
Gate-to-gate
A system boundary that begins at the entry gate of a processing facility and ends at its exit gate, excluding upstream and downstream stages.
IAM-coupled background
A background database whose future factors are derived from Integrated Assessment Models — global energy-economy models used in climate research (for example, the premise tool using SSP/IAM scenarios).
